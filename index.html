01 — Context

The stakes are higher than they look

India's used car market handled roughly five million transactions in FY25, growing faster than new car sales for the third year running. In that market, two digital platforms have moved from niche experiments to genuine scale: Cars24, which built India's first online dealer-auction model starting in 2015, and Spinny, which bet on a vertically integrated C2B2C retail play a few years later. Both want to be the platform a seller trusts when they decide to move on from their car.

The Sell journey is where trust is earned or lost in minutes. A seller arrives with a specific kind of anxiety: they have no reliable idea what their car is worth, they assume dealers will cheat them, and they have likely heard a story from someone who felt robbed by the process. The platform that dissolves the most uncertainty at the right moments wins. The one that creates new uncertainty loses the appointment, the deal, or the customer permanently.

This analysis walks both journeys on the same day using the same vehicle, identifies where friction accumulates, and argues for specific AI interventions that would close the gap. The framework is not who has the better app, but who is losing sellers unnecessarily and where.

02 — Business Models

Two different bets on the same market

The financial trajectories tell the structural story before anything else. Cars24 closed FY25 with revenue of ₹6,233 crore, down 10% year on year, with losses widening slightly to ₹543 crore.[1] Spinny posted ₹4,657 crore in FY25 revenue, up 25%, with losses cut by 28% to ₹423 crore.[2] Cars24 is larger but shrinking. Spinny is smaller but accelerating. Those diverging lines reflect structural choices made years ago about what kind of company each wants to be.

Cars24 is an asset-light auction marketplace. It does not own the cars it buys. A seller gets an indicative price from Cars24's Profecto pricing model, an XGBoost system trained on approximately one million inspections, nine million auction events, and 750,000 transacted cars.[3] After inspection, a live 30-minute online auction runs among 20,000-plus verified dealers, and the highest bid becomes the final offer, valid for 24 hours. Cars24 earns a spread on the transaction, financing income through its Loans24 NBFC arm, and a growing base of paid add-ons including Seller Kavach and Pan-India Pro listing plans.

Spinny runs the opposite bet. It buys as a principal through its SellRight program: an algorithm called the Star Engine sets a price, and that price is contractually guaranteed to land within ±5% of the online estimate after physical inspection.[9] Spinny then refurbishes the car and resells it direct to consumers, capturing the retail margin. Cars that do not meet criteria are declined or routed to the Truebil subsidiary.

04 — Friction Analysis

Five places where sellers disappear

The sell funnel has four natural drop-off points: after the online estimate, after inspection booking, after the actual inspection, and after the price offer. Each one represents real revenue lost. What follows is an analysis of where that loss is happening and why.

01

The price gap that nobody explains

First-hand The same 2024 Tata Nexon received an estimate of ₹8.77L–₹9.53L from Cars24[c5] and ₹6.50L–₹8.04L from Spinny[s11] on the same day. The midpoint gap is approximately ₹1.5 to ₹2 lakh. Neither platform explains how it arrived at its number.

This matters for two reasons. A seller who checks both platforms in the same session, which is common, sees two wildly different numbers and has no basis for trusting either. And Cars24's Profecto model achieves 50% of its predictions within ±5% of final transaction price and 80% within ±10%.[3] That sounds reassuring until you do the arithmetic: the model is wrong by more than 5% on half of all cars. On a ₹9 lakh asset, that is a ₹45,000-plus error band on the optimistic side, and it is precisely this error that materialises as price shock at inspection. The model knows which features drive that variance. Showing that reasoning to the seller is a solvable problem that neither company has solved.

The gap between the two platform estimates also has a structural explanation. Spinny's Star Engine predicts what Spinny itself will pay at a fixed retail margin. Cars24's Profecto predicts what dealers might bid in an open auction. A high-demand variant in a strong local market will fetch more on Cars24; a low-demand car in a weak market will do better on Spinny's guarantee. But the seller has no way to know this without explanation.

02

Sixty percent never book an inspection

A Cars24 product design case study published internally cited that on direct and brand channels, only 43% of users who reach the price estimate page convert to a booked inspection.[6] The remaining 57% leave. The study attributed the drop to two causes: a perceived gap between the quoted price and what sellers expect at inspection, and a complete absence of explanation for how the number was calculated.

This is a trust problem, not a UX problem. The button to book is prominent. The offer is free. The inspector comes to your house. And yet more than half of the people who entered their plate number and saw a price walk away without booking. They are not confused by the interface. They are not confident in the number.

Spinny's intent question[s9] is the only design element on either platform that acknowledges sellers are not a homogeneous group. A seller who says "just checking price" should receive a completely different experience from one who says "immediately." Cars24 treats both identically and loses the browser entirely, when a softer 30-day nurture path might convert them later.

03

The inspection anxiety window

Cars24 tells the seller a car expert will be assigned 30 minutes before the inspection start time.[c8] This is meant to feel efficient. It actually creates a 30-minute window of maximum anxiety: the seller is home, they have readied the car, they do not know who is coming, and they have no ability to confirm or track. Multiple forum threads document inspectors arriving late, cancelling with no notice, or failing to show at all.[10]

The inspection-to-deal conversion rate at comparable C2B platforms has been measured as low as 10%, per JM Financial's analysis of the OLX Autos portfolio before CarTrade shut it down in late 2023.[7] Cars24 and Spinny likely outperform that benchmark, but the documented pattern of price disappointment at inspection suggests the gap is real and large.

Spinny handles this better through two structural choices. The Spinny Hub option[s12] lets a seller drive in and receive a more thorough inspection in a controlled environment. And Spinny communicates evaluation timing as a 45–60 minute window with specific 90-minute slots, which sets clearer expectations than Cars24's single-hour blocks.

04

Cancellation treated as exit, not signal

When a seller cancels on Cars24, they are shown four options[c10]: "I don't want to sell my car right now," "I've sold my car elsewhere," "I'm not available at the scheduled time," and "My reason is not listed." Cars24 stops there. No follow-up, no offer to help, no attempt to understand what went wrong.

Spinny shows eight options[s19] including "Getting better price somewhere else," "Offered price is not attractive," and "Inspector asked to cancel." Those last two name failure modes that are Spinny's fault, not the seller's. More importantly, before the cancellation is confirmed, Spinny surfaces a modal: "Need help with Evaluation? Get a Call Back."[s18] The seller who was about to leave gets an offer of a human conversation instead of a confirmation screen.

Spinny treats cancellation as the start of a retention conversation. Cars24 treats it as a form submission. Eight honest cancellation reasons generate actionable signal. Four polite ones generate noise.

"Inspector asked to cancel" and "Offered price is not attractive" are reasons Spinny lets sellers select. Letting them name the failure is both honest and operationally useful. You cannot fix what you refuse to name.
05

The RC transfer black hole

Both platforms promise free RC (Registration Certificate) transfer after the sale. Both routinely take 60 to 180 days to complete it. In documented cases the delay has cost sellers dearly: challans accumulating in their name from the new owner's driving, a Spinny seller's licence impounded eight months post-sale when ₹50,000-plus in fines accrued, a Bengaluru woman summoned for a hit-and-run she had nothing to do with.[10]

Cars24's Orbit product and its January 2026 acquisition of CarInfo at ₹118 crore signal a serious intent to fix this at the data layer — CarInfo brings 12 million monthly active users of vehicle data, with live lookups for registration, insurance, PUC, FASTag, and challan status.[5] But the seller-facing communication around RC status remains opaque on both platforms. The seller receives their money on day one and then has no reliable way to know whether they are still legally exposed months later.

05 — AI Strategy

Where AI closes the gap

Cars24 has the most publicly disclosed AI stack in Indian used cars by a wide margin: Profecto for price prediction, Profundus for computer vision at inspection, Autonaut as a RAG-based consumer chatbot at 36% pilot adoption,[8] a self-hosted PaddleOCR document pipeline, and an ElevenLabs voice-agent deployment now handling roughly 3 million minutes of outbound calls monthly with reported +35% conversion and −50% calling costs.[4] Spinny's public AI disclosure is nearly nonexistent in comparison.

The gap that neither platform has closed is pre-inspection: using AI to align seller expectations with reality before the inspector ever arrives. That is where 57–60% of the funnel is lost. Each intervention below maps directly to that problem and to architecture that has been built and run in production.

01 Addresses Friction 1 + 2
Pre-inspection condition elicitor agent
A conversational agent that runs between the price estimate and the inspection booking. It asks the seller about their car's actual condition — any dents, service history gaps, accident disclosures. Each answer feeds back into the price range in real time with a one-line explanation: "Your declared front bumper dent typically reduces value by ₹8,000–₹12,000 at inspection." The seller arrives with a tighter, more honest number. The inspector arrives with a pre-declared baseline. Price-shock cancellations fall. This is a LangGraph multi-step agent: one node per question category, memory carried across the session, and a final price-adjustment summary as output. Cars24's Profecto already produces the SHAP values that explain each feature's weight. The missing piece is surfacing them in natural language.
I have built this
The JindalX real-time claim assistant is architecturally identical: a stateful LangGraph agent that gathers structured inputs in a live session, feeds them into a downstream decision model, and returns a natural-language decision — deployed across 200+ concurrent revenue-cycle agents. The Cars24 version swaps claim fields for condition signals and the insurance payor API for Profecto. The orchestration pattern is the same.
02 Addresses Friction 2
Intent-aware journey routing
Spinny's "When are you planning to sell?" question is the only intent signal captured on either platform, and Spinny does nothing with it beyond collecting it. A seller who says "just checking" should enter a 30-day nurture sequence with weekly market-movement alerts for their specific car. A seller who says "immediately" should get the first available inspection slot and a pre-filled document upload prompt. Routing on stated intent requires no new infrastructure, only a branching architecture on top of Cars24's existing stack.
I have built this
The 25% accuracy improvement on Clari's sales copilot came from upstream signal classification — categorising prospect intent from sparse input, then routing to the appropriate retrieval and response strategy. That is the same problem: one early signal, branching downstream experiences, measurable conversion impact. The model is a single-question intent classifier. The hard part is building the content of each nurture path, not the routing logic.
03 Addresses Friction 3
Proactive pre-inspection voice agent
Cars24 already runs ElevenLabs voice agents for outbound post-booking calls.[4] Extending that same pipeline to a pre-inspection confirmation call — sent 60 minutes before the slot with the inspector's name, ETA, and a checklist of what the seller should have ready — eliminates the 30-minute anxiety window without changing operations. During the inspection itself, a WhatsApp progress update ("Exterior evaluation complete — moving to engine bay") anchors the seller to the process. This is an orchestration change to an existing system, not a new system.
I have built this
The SisterAgency auto pipeline chains two agents end-to-end with WebSocket-based real-time messaging at each step. The pattern — trigger on event, run agent, push status to user in real time, hand off on completion — is the same one needed here. The Cars24 version triggers on appointment confirmation, runs the voice agent, and sends WhatsApp updates through the inspection lifecycle.
04 Addresses Friction 4
Cancellation winback agent
Spinny's "Get a Call Back" modal is the right instinct deployed manually. An AI version reads the cancellation reason selected, references the specific car and quote, and opens with a targeted counter: "You mentioned the price wasn't attractive. Based on your car's Dark Edition trim and current Noida dealer demand, ₹9.1L is achievable through auction. Would you like to reschedule?" That is not a generic save attempt. It is a data-driven response to a stated objection. Cars24 has the auction-demand signal. The agent architecture reads the cancellation event, retrieves relevant pricing context, delivers a targeted response, and escalates to a human if the conversation continues beyond two exchanges.
I have built this
The JindalX claim assistant is triggered automatically on claim creation and responds in real time within the same session — no queued follow-up, no batch processing. The winback agent is the same pattern: event-triggered, context-retrieved, session-bound, with a human escalation path. The difference is the trigger (cancellation rather than claim creation) and the retrieval source (Cars24 auction demand rather than payor eligibility data).
05 Addresses Friction 5
RC transfer status intelligence layer
Cars24's CarInfo acquisition gives it live access to VAHAN, hypothecation NOC status, challan lookups, and Form-35 filing status across all RTOs.[5] The missing piece is a seller-facing communication layer that surfaces this data proactively. A weekly WhatsApp message stating "RC transfer is pending at Lucknow RTO — current processing time in that office is 45 days, you are at day 22" is not technically complex. It requires connecting CarInfo data to a notification pipeline and a natural language status template. The result is a seller who knows they are not exposed rather than one who assumes the worst.
I have built this
The Azure OpenAI eligibility pipeline at JindalX processes 1,000-plus records a day at 99%+ accuracy: structured data from payor APIs goes in, natural language eligibility status comes out, delivered with no human in the loop per record. Cutting manual effort by 60% came from applying the same transformation to every record at scale. The RC status layer is the same architecture: structured data in (CarInfo VAHAN API), natural language status out, delivered weekly to every post-sale seller.
06 — Conclusion

The real competition is for seller trust, not seller volume

Cars24 and Spinny are not running the same company with different names. They are running structurally different experiments on the same human moment: the moment a person decides to hand over something they have owned and cared for, and trust they will be treated fairly.

Cars24 wins on speed, geographic reach, and the depth of its AI stack. Spinny wins on price certainty, post-booking cleanliness, and the honesty of its cancellation design. The place where both companies lose is the same: between the online estimate and the inspection appointment. That 57–60% drop-off is not a design problem. It is a trust problem. And trust problems in 2026 are AI problems, because the technology to explain a price, personalise a journey, predict a cancellation, and automate a follow-up is no longer research — it is production.

The five interventions above are not speculative. Each one maps directly to a system already running in production: the JindalX claim assistant, the Azure OpenAI eligibility pipeline, the Clari retrieval and routing work, the SisterAgency real-time pipeline architecture. The engineering patterns are known. The question is whether the product organisation is aligned around the right problem.

The right problem is not getting more people to enter their plate number. It is making the person who entered their plate number trust you enough to show up when the inspector rings the doorbell.
